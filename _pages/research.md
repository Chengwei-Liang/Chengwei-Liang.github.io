My research focuses on fast radio bursts (FRBs), one of the most enigmatic classes of radio transients. I study them observationally, combining radio follow-up, polarization measurements, and machine learning to understand how they are produced, where they live, and why some of them repeat.

## Observational Studies of FRBs

FRBs are millisecond-duration bursts of coherent radio emission that can briefly outshine their host galaxies, yet their physical origin remains an open question. Their dispersion measures (DMs) and rotation measures (RMs) make them sensitive probes of the ionized and magnetized material along the line of sight, so long-term monitoring of active sources offers a rare opportunity to follow how their local environments evolve.

As principal investigator, I have secured more than 100 hours of observing time on FAST and the Parkes (Murriyang) telescope through several FRB follow-up programs. I lead the data reduction and analysis, and I use the resulting timing and polarimetric measurements to track the evolution of DM and RM in repeating sources.

In April 2026, FRB 20180301A entered a renewed active phase. We obtained a sample of bursts and measured updated DM and RM values, providing a fresh view of the source's changing magneto-ionic environment. The analysis of this active episode is ongoing.

## FRB Progenitors and Persistent Radio Sources

A growing number of FRBs appear to be associated with persistent radio sources (PRSs): compact, luminous radio counterparts that may trace young magnetars or embedded supernova remnants. Although the PRS–FRB connection is increasingly discussed, its physical nature and prevalence remain uncertain, and nearby PRS-like sources provide a valuable testbed for these ideas.

We carried out 98 hours of radio observations of VT 1137−0337, a PRS-like source. No FRB-like emission was detected. I led the interpretation of the non-detection and its implications for the relationship between PRSs and FRBs.

The non-detection allows us to place useful constraints on the burst rate and radio luminosity of VT 1137−0337, and it suggests that not every PRS-like source is necessarily an active FRB engine. A paper reporting this work is in preparation.

## Classification of Repeating and Non-repeating FRBs

Whether repeating and apparently non-repeating FRBs belong to the same population, or whether they represent physically distinct classes of sources, is one of the central questions in the field. Most comparisons have relied on timing and spectral properties; polarization offers an independent and potentially more discriminating view of the emission mechanism and the magneto-ionic environment.

Using machine-learning and deep-learning methods, I am exploring whether the two populations differ in their polarization properties. A distinctive aspect of this work is that the models are trained on polarization data rather than on standard timing or spectral parameters, allowing us to test whether the two classes separate in polarized emission alone.

This work is carried out as part of a collaborative project within the FAST FRB KSP, and I have presented it in two oral talks. The analysis is ongoing, with the aim of clarifying whether polarization provides a robust route to distinguishing the two populations.
